# 05 - Audience and register

The audience decision is not one decision among many. It is the input to almost every other decision
in this folder. Made explicitly, it settles a dozen arguments in advance; left implicit, it gets
made anyway, inconsistently, chapter by chapter.

Citation keys resolve in [`sources.md`](sources.md).

---

## The candidates

**A. The curious general reader.** No philosophy background. Picked the book up out of interest, will
not read the primary texts, and will stop if a chapter is hard work without a visible payoff. The
audience of [Warburton 2011], [Durant 1926] and [Grayling 2019].

**B. The serious self-learner.** No formal training, but motivated and intends to go further. Wants a
map of the territory, a sense of which arguments actually matter, and a reading path into the
primary texts. Will tolerate difficulty if told it is coming and why. Broadly the audience of
[Gottlieb 2000] and of [Adamson 2014- ].

**C. The student.** Reading alongside a course, needs coverage that matches a syllabus and summaries
that survive an exam. The audience [Kenny 2004-2007] and [Copleston 1946-1975] serve.

These are not degrees of the same thing. B is not "A but more so": B wants different apparatus, a
different chapter length and a different honesty about difficulty. Trying to serve all three
produces a book that serves none, which is the usual way this decision fails (synthesis).

Two rows deliberately do not vary. Proportion between traditions and the era-labelling rule are
matters of accuracy, not of pitch: a general-reader book has no more licence to give one tradition
an eighth of the space while claiming to cover five than a scholarly one does. That is the failure
[Humphreys 2019] identifies in [Grayling 2019], and [Garfield & Van Norden 2016] make the general
point that scope should be stated honestly whoever the reader is.

## Recommendation

**Proposal, not a finding. The author decides this, and everything above is usable with a different
answer substituted.**

**Write for B, the serious self-learner, with A's readability as a constraint rather than a target.**
Concretely: a reader with no philosophy background who intends to read some philosophy afterwards.

The reasoning:

1. **C is not available for this book.** The scope is global and multi-tradition. Almost no course is
   organised that way, so there is no syllabus to align with, and writing for students would mean
   inventing the course as well as the book.

2. **A collides with the format.** The general-reader pitch pushes toward many short chapters, and
   the arithmetic in `04` is unforgiving: past a certain chapter count there is no room for slot 4,
   and without slot 4 the book is a list of positions rather than of arguments, which is the
   doxography [Rorty 1984] identifies as the genre to avoid. [Warburton 2011] accepts that trade
   knowingly and executes it well. This book, spanning several traditions, would have to accept it
   much harder.

3. **B is what the multi-tradition scope is actually for.** A reader who only wants the gist is well
   served by existing Western surveys. The reason to write a global history is to give someone a map
   they cannot currently get, and map-users are readers who intend to travel.

4. **B makes the apparatus coherent.** Annotated further reading, the tradition-grouped glossary, the
   thread guide and the parallel timeline are all substantial investments recommended in `04`. For
   A they are decoration. For B they are half the value of the book.

5. **A as a constraint keeps B honest.** Writing for the self-learner is not licence to write for
   colleagues. The prose standard stays general-reader: no unexplained term, no assumed background,
   nothing that requires a previous chapter to have been read closely. [Pinker 2014] is the standing
   warning here, since the curse of knowledge is what turns a book for motivated non-specialists
   into a book for specialists without the author noticing.

**What this settles.** Read the B column of the propagation table as the default for every decision
in this folder.

**What follows in practice:** fewer, longer chapters over more, shorter ones; the argument gets the
space; difficulty is named rather than smoothed; every chapter ends with somewhere to go next.

## Register

Given B:

- **Assume no philosophy.** Not one term, not one figure, not one distinction. Assume ordinary adult
  general knowledge and normal curiosity.
- **Assume willingness to work, and earn it.** This reader will follow a hard argument if told it is
  hard, told why it matters, and taken through it. What they will not forgive is being made to work
  for something the author could have said plainly.
- **Explain before you name.** Give the idea, then the term for it. Never the reverse.
- **Prefer the concrete case.** An example before the abstraction, every time.
- **Plain sentences carrying difficult content.** The difficulty should live in the ideas, never in
  the prose. If a sentence has to be read twice, it should be because the thought is hard.
- **A voice is allowed.** Judgement, wit and enthusiasm are assets, and [Russell 1945] is still read
  because it has them. What is not allowed is judgement in the exposition register, which is the
  documented charge against that same book. See `03`.
- **Second person sparingly, first person deliberately.** "I think Aquinas fails here" is honest and
  correctly marks the register. Use it for assessment; keep it out of exposition.
- **No flattery of the reader, no apology for the subject.** Neither "as you will brilliantly see"
  nor "this dry material".

### Who the book is not for

Worth stating in the introduction in a sentence or two. It is not a reference work, not a textbook,
and not a substitute for the primary texts. Saying so sets expectations correctly and costs nothing,
and it is the same honesty about scope that [Garfield & Van Norden 2016] ask for.
