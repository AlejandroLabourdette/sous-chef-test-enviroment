# 04 - Chapter shape, length, apparatus and voice

The mechanics. How a chapter is built, how long things are, what goes in the back of the book, and
how the prose stays recognisably one book across dozens of subjects. Citation keys resolve in
[`sources.md`](sources.md).

---

## Why a fixed chapter template

In a book of forty or more chapters written over a long period, a repeatable template is not a
constraint on the writing, it is the mechanism that makes the rest of the guidance enforceable
(synthesis):

- It **makes proportion measurable.** You cannot check the balance of the book if every chapter has
  a different shape.
- It **caps biography structurally.** If biography has a named slot, it cannot quietly eat the
  chapter. See the context budget in [`02-chronological-format.md`](02-chronological-format.md).
- It **forces continuity.** If "what this thinker inherited" and "what they handed on" are required
  slots, no chapter can float free of the chronology.
- It **trains the reader.** By chapter four they know where to find the argument, which is what lets
  the chapters be short without being thin.
- It **stops the register from leaking.** A named slot for assessment keeps judgement out of the
  exposition slots. See [`03-faithful-exposition.md`](03-faithful-exposition.md).

[Warburton 2011] demonstrates the payoff at the short end: forty brief chapters from Socrates to
Peter Singer, readable straight through because they are built the same way.

---

## The chapter template

Seven slots. Proportions are of chapter length, not fixed word counts, and they sum to 100% because
each slot is inclusive of its own connective prose. The transitions, framing and signposting the rest
of this folder asks for come out of the slot they serve, not from a separate budget.

| # | Slot | Share | Contents |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The inherited problem** | ~10% | The question left open, and by whom. Opens the chapter inside the chronology rather than with a birth date |
| 2 | **The person, briefly** | ~10% | Only what bears on the thought, or one vivid detail that buys attention. Hard cap |
| 3 | **The central move** | ~10% | The one idea, stated plainly, before any machinery. If it cannot be said in a paragraph, the chapter has not found its subject |
| 4 | **The argument** | ~40% | The reasoning, reconstructed so the reader could argue it back. The heart of the chapter and the reason the book exists |
| 5 | **The cost** | ~15% | The strongest objection, what the position gives up, and what its holder accepted in exchange. Where the difficulty is preserved |
| 6 | **What it left behind** | ~10% | The problem handed on, and to whom. Sets up a later chapter |
| 7 | **Where to start reading** | ~5% | Two or three items, each with one sentence saying why and how hard it is |

Slots 1 and 6 are the chronological hinges. If they are weak across the book, the order is doing no
work and the material should have been organised by topic instead.

**Where the template flexes.** Chapters whose subject is a school, a text or a translation movement
rather than a person (recommended in `02`) replace slot 2 with a short account of the setting and
who was involved. Two thinkers in genuine dispute can share a chapter with slots 3 to 5 run twice.
Anything else that wants to break the template is a signal to check whether it should.

## Length

Length is the decision that most constrains everything else, and it is downstream of audience. See
[`05-audience-and-register.md`](05-audience-and-register.md).

The exemplars bracket the range:

| Book | Shape | What the length buys and costs |
|---|---|---|
| [Warburton 2011] | 40 short chapters, one volume | Readable end to end. A thinker gets one or two ideas and no depth |
| [Grayling 2019] | 704 pages, one volume, Western plus four other traditions | One volume covering the world. Everything outside the West compressed into about an eighth of it in total, which [Humphreys 2019] calls tokenistic |
| [Gottlieb 2000] | Multi-volume, general audience | Room for arguments to stay intact. The series is still incomplete |
| [Kenny 2004-2007] | 4 volumes, narrative plus thematic | Both axes covered. Roughly doubles the length, with deliberate overlap |
| [Copleston 1946-1975] | 9 volumes | Nothing omitted. No general reader will finish it |

**The arithmetic that matters.** Total length divided by number of thinkers gives the per-chapter
budget, and it is unforgiving. Cover eighty thinkers in 150,000 words and each gets under 1,900
words, which is enough for slot 3 and a gesture at slot 4. Cover forty in the same space and each
gets 3,750, which is enough to reconstruct an argument properly.

**Recommendation (judgement):** decide the number of chapters from the word budget and the minimum
viable chapter, not the other way round. A chapter that cannot fit slot 4 at a defensible size
should not be a chapter; fold that thinker into a neighbouring chapter as part of a debate, or into
the era opening. **Breadth is bought with the only thing that makes the book worth reading.**

## The thematic axis

Chronology fragments every theme. A reader who wants to follow what happened to the problem of
free will has to reassemble it from twelve chapters in four parts.

[Kenny 2004-2007] solves this directly: each volume runs a chronological narrative and then revisits
the same period thematically, through chapters on logic and language, knowledge, physics,
metaphysics, mind and soul, ethics, politics, aesthetics and God, with the volumes designed to
dovetail so a theme can be traced across centuries. It works, and it roughly doubles the book.

**Recommendation (judgement):** do not duplicate Kenny's structure in a single-volume general book.
Get most of the benefit for a fraction of the length with two lighter devices:

- **The era-closing synchronic section** already proposed in `02`, which carries themes across
  traditions within an era.
- **A thread guide in the back matter**: six to ten named threads (the self, knowledge, the good
  life, the state, what exists, language, the divine), each a short paragraph plus an ordered list of
  the chapters and sections that carry it. Perhaps three pages total, and it turns the book into
  something that can be read along either axis.

**Cost:** the thread guide is a navigation aid, not an argument. It does not give the reader the
synthesis Kenny's thematic chapters actually write. If the book has room for one substantive
thematic chapter per era instead, that is strictly better and considerably longer.

## Apparatus

Each item with a recommendation and its cost.

### Timeline
**Recommend: yes, and as parallel columns, one per tradition, on a shared date axis.**
For a multi-tradition book this is not decoration, it is the one place simultaneity becomes visible
at a glance, and it does structurally what the prose can only do sequentially. [Grayling 2019]
includes a timeline of philosophers.
**Cost:** parallel columns force decisions about dating that the prose can leave vague, including
for composite and undated texts (see O3 in `02`). Mark uncertain dates visibly rather than silently
picking one.

### Glossary
**Recommend: yes, organised by tradition rather than alphabetically as one list.**
The book has two distinct vocabulary problems: transliterated terms with no English equivalent
(*dharma*, *li*, *ren*, *logos*, *ousia*), and English words that drift between centuries and
traditions (idea, nature, virtue, reason, substance). Both are the curse of knowledge in
[Pinker 2014] made concrete. A tradition-grouped glossary lets an entry say what the word means
*here*, which a single alphabetical list cannot without repetition.
**Cost:** harder to look up cold. Mitigate with an alphabetical index of terms pointing into the
grouped glossary.
**Note:** the glossary does not excuse the chapter. Every load-bearing term is still glossed at
first use in the text, per `03`.

### Further reading
**Recommend: per chapter, annotated, at the end of the chapter, not pooled at the back.**
Two or three items, each with a sentence on what it is and how hard it is. This is slot 7 of the
template, and it is what makes the book a door rather than a wall (P9 in
[`01-what-makes-it-good.md`](01-what-makes-it-good.md)). A bare list of titles at the back is
apparatus theatre: it looks like guidance and gives none.
**Cost:** a few hundred words per chapter, and it dates faster than the rest of the book.

### Citations
**Recommend: endnotes keyed by page and phrase, no note markers in the body text.**
A general reader is stopped by superscript numbers; a checking reader is served fine by an endnote
section keyed to page and quoted phrase. Quotations, contested readings, statistics and any claim
about what a scholar thinks all get a note. Ordinary exposition does not.
**Cost:** slightly harder for the checking reader to know a note exists.
**Revisited against the audience recommendation in `05`, which is B, the serious self-learner, not
the general reader.** The recommendation survives but the reasoning changes: this reader *is* the
checking reader, so the notes matter more, and the B column of the propagation table asks for
"endnotes, denser". Keep note markers out of the body text, because unbroken prose is still what
makes a hard argument readable, but treat the endnote section as load-bearing apparatus rather than
as a concession: keyed by page and phrase, generous rather than minimal, and pointing at editions
this reader can actually get hold of.
**Non-negotiable:** every translation used is named, per `03`.

### Pronunciation guide
**Recommend: yes, short, for transliterated names and terms.**
Specific to the multi-tradition scope, and cheap. A reader who cannot say *Nagarjuna*, *Zhuangzi* or
*Ibn Rushd* in their head reads those chapters with more friction than the Greek ones, which
quietly reinforces the imbalance the book is trying to avoid.
**Cost:** one or two pages, and approximations are always imperfect. Say so.

### Note on scope and canon
**Recommend: yes, a short front or back section saying who is in the book and why, and who is not.**
[Park 2013] shows the familiar canon was actively reshaped between 1780 and 1830 on grounds that
included a racialised philosophical anthropology, and [Garfield & Van Norden 2016] argue that scope
should be stated honestly rather than left implicit. A book that silently reproduces an inherited
list has made a claim without defending it.
**Cost:** it invites the argument. That is the point.

### Index
**Recommend: yes, names and concepts.** Standard for the genre; [Grayling 2019] carries one.
**Cost:** it is real work, usually late and usually professional.

## Keeping one voice across many thinkers

The specific risk is that each chapter absorbs its subject. The Nietzsche chapter turns aphoristic,
the Hegel chapter turns opaque, the Wittgenstein chapter turns gnomic, and the book stops being one
book. Rendering a philosopher's style is not the same as explaining their thought, and it usually
substitutes for it.

Practical measures (synthesis):

1. **The template does most of the work.** Same slots, same order, every chapter.
2. **Fix the register signals once** and reuse them everywhere. The phrases in `03` should be the
   same phrases in chapter 2 and chapter 40.
3. **Read the openings alone.** Extract slot 1 from every chapter and read them consecutively. Drift
   in tone, in tense, in how much is assumed, shows up immediately.
4. **Read the closings alone.** Same for slots 6 and 7. This also catches the escalator, since a run
   of closings that each point forward to something better is a book claiming progress.
5. **Keep a style sheet** from chapter one: transliteration scheme, BCE or BC, how dates are given,
   how titles are cited, which spellings, how untranslated terms are set. Amend it deliberately, and
   never silently.
6. **Quote sparingly and consistently.** A fixed policy on quotation length and on when to quote at
   all prevents chapters where the philosopher's voice crowds out the author's.
7. **Consistent difficulty, not consistent simplicity.** Some thinkers are harder, and the book
   should be honest about that (P3 in `01`). What must not vary is how much the book helps: the same
   level of scaffolding everywhere, applied to material of different difficulty.

## Checklist

**Chapter**
- [ ] Are all seven slots present, or is the deviation deliberate and noted?
- [ ] Is slot 4 the largest section?
- [ ] Do slots 1 and 6 name specific predecessors and successors, not "earlier thinkers"?
- [ ] Is slot 2 inside its cap?
- [ ] Does slot 7 give reasons, not just titles?

**Length**
- [ ] Is the chapter count derived from the word budget and the minimum viable chapter?
- [ ] Does any chapter fail to fit slot 4 at a defensible size? If so, has it been merged?
- [ ] Measured, not estimated: does the per-tradition share match the rule set in `02`?

**Apparatus**
- [ ] Timeline: parallel columns, uncertain dates visibly marked?
- [ ] Glossary: grouped by tradition, with an alphabetical index into it?
- [ ] Further reading: per chapter, annotated with reasons?
- [ ] Citations: every quotation, contested reading and attributed scholarly view noted? Every
      translation named?
- [ ] Pronunciation guide present, with its approximations acknowledged?
- [ ] Is there a stated note on scope and canon?

**Voice**
- [ ] Do the openings read as one book when read consecutively?
- [ ] Do the closings, and do they avoid implying progress?
- [ ] Does any chapter imitate its subject's style rather than explain it?
- [ ] Is the style sheet current?
