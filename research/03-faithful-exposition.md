# 03 - Presenting a philosopher faithfully

How to get someone's thought onto the page without misrepresenting it, when you have a few thousand
words and they had a lifetime. Citation keys resolve in [`sources.md`](sources.md).

---

## The frame: know which genre you are writing

[Rorty 1984] distinguishes four ways of writing about past philosophy, and the most useful thing
about the distinction is that it lets you ask, of any given paragraph, which one you are doing.

| Genre | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Historical reconstruction** | What did this thinker mean, in their own terms, to their own contemporaries? |
| **Rational reconstruction** | Treated as a contemporary, what is this thinker saying to us, and is it right? |
| **Geistesgeschichte** | How did we come to be asking the questions we now ask? This is what justifies a canon |
| **Doxography** | Who are the famous philosophers and what did they think? The genre to avoid |

Rorty's central point is that the choice between the first two is a **false dilemma**: both are
necessary. A book that only does historical reconstruction is an antiquarian exercise with no reason
for a general reader to care. A book that only does rational reconstruction produces the
[Russell 1945] problem, where past thinkers become opponents in a present-day argument and stop
being themselves.

**The working rule:** do both, in that order, visibly separated. Establish what the thinker meant
before asking what it is worth. The reader should always be able to tell which one they are reading.

## Drawing the line on the page

**Attribution.** The three-register scheme and the signal-phrase table below are this folder's own
`(synthesis)`. The problem they solve is documented - it is the charge against [Russell 1945] stated
by [Symposium 1947] - but no source proposes this scheme as the remedy. It is a working device, not a
finding.

There are three registers, and the whole problem is keeping them distinguishable.

1. **Exposition.** What the text says or claims. Defensible by citation.
2. **Interpretation.** A reading that goes beyond what the text states, and that a competent scholar
   could dispute.
3. **Assessment.** Your judgement about whether it works.

All three belong in the book. [Copleston 1946-1975] is thorough on 1 and 2; [Russell 1945] is
enjoyable on 3 and is criticised precisely for letting 3 leak into 1, so that the account is "not
always accurate in its account of the thinkers it covers, nor always fair to them".

**Rule: never let interpretation or assessment appear in the grammatical voice of exposition.**
Signal the register in the sentence itself.

| Register | Signal phrases |
|---|---|
| Exposition | "Aristotle argues that...", "In the *Republic*, Plato has Socrates claim..." |
| Interpretation | "The most defensible reading of this passage is...", "This is usually taken to mean...", "Read alongside the later chapters, the claim looks like..." |
| Assessment | "This seems to me to fail because...", "The obvious objection is..." |

The failure is not having opinions. It is the unmarked slide, where "Aristotle argues that" is
followed three sentences later by an inference the reader has no way of attributing.

**A test for a finished chapter:** highlight every sentence that is not plain exposition. If the
highlighted sentences do not carry a signal phrase, the register is leaking.

## Charity, and where it stops

The principle: interpret so as to take the strongest available reading rather than attributing
irrationality or obvious falsehood where a coherent reading exists. Quine's formulation is that an
interlocutor's silliness, beyond a certain point, is less likely than bad translation
[Quine 1960]; Davidson's is that we make maximum sense of others when we interpret in a way that
optimises agreement.

For a historical survey this does real work, because the naive reading of a text from 400 BCE or
1200 CE is almost always the one that makes its author look foolish, and it is usually the one a
reader will reach on their own.

Charity has three limits, and each is a real constraint. The principle itself is [Quine 1960]; the
limits C1-C3 are `(synthesis)`, drawn from the tension between charity and [Skinner 1969]:

**C1. Charity governs reading, not verdict.** Take the strongest version of the position, then say
what you think of it. Charity is not a reason to conclude that everybody was right. It raises the
standard of the argument you have to answer; it does not excuse you from answering.

**C2. Charity is bounded by the text.** Pushed past the evidence, charity turns into modernising:
the position gets improved until it is no longer the historical one, and the reader is told that a
thirteenth-century argument survives an objection it never met. This is where charity collides with
[Skinner 1969], whose **mythology of coherence** is exactly what over-charitable reading produces,
a tidy system imposed on work that had none. **Rule:** if the charitable reading requires a premise
the thinker never states, it is your reconstruction, and it moves to the interpretation register.

**C3. Charity is not exculpation.** Philosophers in the canon held views that are indefensible:
about slavery, about women, about other peoples. Charity means getting those positions right, in
their own terms, including their reasons. It does not mean minimising them, and it does not mean a
respectful silence. [Park 2013] is a reminder of what is at stake, documenting how a racialised
philosophical anthropology in Kant, Hegel and their contemporaries shaped the discipline's own
account of itself. State the position accurately, state its grounds, and state your view. That is
all three registers doing their job.

## Primary sources and translation

**Attribution.** `(synthesis)` throughout, except where cited. The rules follow from the curse of
knowledge in [Pinker 2014] and the interpretive stakes in [Skinner 1969], but the specific policies
below are the folder's own.

**Work from the primary texts.** A survey assembled from other surveys inherits every error in them
and adds compression. [Gottlieb 2000] is the accessible model of a general-audience history written
from the texts.

**Name the translation you used.** For most of this book the primary text is a translation, and
translation choices are interpretive choices. A reader who wants to check you needs to know which
edition to open.

**Do not translate a load-bearing term into a word that carries the wrong freight.** *Logos*,
*ousia*, *dharma*, *li*, *ren*, *dao*, *nous* and their equivalents do not have English
counterparts, and the available English words (reason, substance, duty, principle, benevolence, way,
mind) all import associations the original does not have.

**Rule:** where a term is load-bearing and does not map, keep it transliterated, gloss it once at
first use with the range of meanings rather than a single equivalent, and say plainly that the
standard English rendering is misleading. Where the term is incidental, translate it and move on.
This connects directly to the curse of knowledge in [Pinker 2014]: the writer stops noticing that a
term is technical long before the reader does.

**Watch for words that drift.** *Idea*, *nature*, *virtue*, *science*, *reason* and *substance* all
mean different things in different centuries and traditions. In a book that crosses both, the same
English word will appear in a dozen chapters meaning a dozen things. See the glossary discussion in
[`04-structure-and-apparatus.md`](04-structure-and-apparatus.md).

## Scholarly disagreement

Specialists disagree about what these texts mean, sometimes fundamentally. A survey that presents
one reading with total confidence is not simpler, it is less accurate.

But acknowledging every dispute would make the book unreadable, so the question is which disputes
surface. A workable triage (synthesis):

- **The dispute changes what the reader takes away.** Surface it. Name that there is a dispute,
  characterise the two sides in a sentence each, say which reading the book uses and why. This costs
  a paragraph and buys the reader accuracy plus a genuine glimpse of how the field works.
- **The dispute is real but does not change the reader's takeaway.** Choose a reading, use it, and
  put the dispute in further reading or a note. Do not pretend it does not exist; do not spend
  chapter space on it.
- **The dispute is about details of scholarship rather than about the philosophy.** Leave it out
  entirely.

The triage is `(judgement)`. Nothing in the sources says which disputes a survey should surface.

Two things to avoid. First, false balance: not every reading has a serious constituency, and
presenting a fringe view as an equal side misinforms. Second, borrowed confidence: if the honest
answer is that nobody knows what a passage means, say so. "The passage is obscure and has been read
in at least three incompatible ways" is a true and interesting sentence.

## Failure modes

| Failure | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Unmarked slide** | Exposition and assessment in one undifferentiated voice; the reader cannot attribute a claim |
| **Ventriloquism** | The author's argument delivered in the philosopher's name, per the charge against [Russell 1945] |
| **Modernising** | Charity extended past the evidence until the position is no longer the historical one |
| **Manufactured coherence** | An inconsistent body of work smoothed into a system, the mythology of coherence in [Skinner 1969] |
| **Prolepsis** | The thinker described as anticipating a later doctrine, per [Skinner 1969] |
| **The single translation** | A key term rendered by one English word that carries the wrong associations, silently |
| **Borrowed confidence** | A contested reading presented as settled, because hedging felt weak |
| **False balance** | A fringe reading given equal weight with the scholarly mainstream |
| **Exculpation** | An indefensible historical view softened or skipped in the name of respect |

## Checklist

Run against each chapter draft.

**Register**
- [ ] Can every claim be sorted into exposition, interpretation or assessment?
- [ ] Does every interpretation and assessment sentence carry a signal phrase?
- [ ] Does the chapter do historical reconstruction before rational reconstruction, and are the two
      distinguishable?

**Charity**
- [ ] Is the version of the position presented the strongest one available?
- [ ] Does the charitable reading rely on any premise the thinker never states? If so, is it flagged
      as reconstruction?
- [ ] Where the thinker held views that are indefensible, are they stated accurately rather than
      softened or omitted?

**Sources**
- [ ] Is this chapter built from primary texts rather than from other surveys?
- [ ] Is the translation named?
- [ ] Is every load-bearing untranslatable term kept in the original and glossed at first use?
- [ ] Has any drifting term (idea, nature, virtue, reason, substance) been used without saying which
      sense is meant here?

**Disagreement**
- [ ] Is every dispute that changes the reader's takeaway surfaced, with sides named and a stated
      choice?
- [ ] Is anything presented as settled that is not?
- [ ] Is any fringe reading given mainstream weight?
