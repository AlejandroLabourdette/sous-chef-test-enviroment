# 01 - What makes a philosophy book good

The question is not which books are enjoyable. It is which properties make a book of this kind
succeed or fail at its job, so that a draft can be checked against them. Each principle below states
the rule, why it holds, and what it is grounded in. Citation keys resolve in [`sources.md`](sources.md).

---

## Principles

### P1. Give the reader the argument, not just the position

A philosopher's conclusion without the reasoning that got there is trivia. The reader learns that
Hume denied necessary connection, but cannot see why anyone would, cannot evaluate it, and cannot
disagree with it. The test is whether a reader who finishes the section could argue the position
back at you, and could say where they think it goes wrong.

This is the difference [Rorty 1984] draws between real historiography and **doxography**: running
down a list of famous names and summarising their views. He treats doxography as the genre to
avoid, and describes it as mummifying famous philosophers. A chronological survey of great thinkers
is the format most at risk of it, because the list of names comes pre-supplied and summarising each
one feels like the assignment.

### P2. Keep your own position separable from your subject's

The reader must always be able to tell what the philosopher claimed from what you think about it.
Both can be on the page. They cannot be in the same sentence unmarked.

This is the documented charge against [Russell 1945]: it "is not always accurate in its account of
the thinkers it covers, nor always fair to them", and a symposium on the work put the objection
precisely, that a history of philosophy should be an objective statement of doctrines and arguments,
whereas Russell commented on the history of ideas from his own point of view. [Copleston 1946-1975]
was written in part to redress this, aiming to give full place to each thinker and present the
thought in a rounded way.

Note that the fix is not neutrality. Russell's book is readable *because* he has a view. The fix is
marking the boundary, so the reader can take your judgement or leave it and still get the philosopher.

### P3. Preserve the difficulty

Some philosophy is hard because it is badly written, and that can be cleared away. Some is hard
because the problem is hard, and clearing that away is a lie. If the reader closes the chapter
feeling that Kant was straightforward, the chapter has misinformed them.

The practical form: when an argument has a genuine weak joint, or when a position has a cost its
holder accepted, say so rather than presenting a frictionless summary. (synthesis, from the contrast
between [Copleston 1946-1975] and the popular-survey approach of [Durant 1926])

### P4. Show what each thinker inherited and what they handed on

An idea presented in isolation is an opinion. The same idea presented as a response to a live
problem someone else had left open becomes intelligible. This is the property [Copleston 1946-1975]
was praised for: showing each thinker's links to those who went before and those who came after.

For a chronological book this is not a nice extra, it is the reason the format exists. If the
chapters could be shuffled without loss, chronology is doing no work and the book should have been
organised by topic instead. See [`02-chronological-format.md`](02-chronological-format.md).

### P5. Interpret charitably, by default and on the record

Take the strongest available reading of a position rather than the one easiest to dismiss. The
principle of charity in its standard form: interpret so as not to attribute irrationality or obvious
falsehood where a coherent reading exists. Quine's version is that an interlocutor's silliness,
beyond a certain point, is less likely than bad translation [Quine 1960].

For a historical survey this has teeth, because the easiest reading of a text from 400 BCE or 1200
CE is usually the one that makes its author look naive. Charity has limits, and the limits are
worked out in [`03-faithful-exposition.md`](03-faithful-exposition.md).

### P6. Say what the book covers, on the cover

Scope is a choice, and the reader is owed it explicitly rather than by omission. This is the whole
argument of [Garfield & Van Norden 2016]: a curriculum that covers only the European canon should
say so in its name rather than call itself philosophy without qualification. The same applies to a
book title.

The failure is not narrow scope. It is a broad claim delivering a narrow book. [Grayling 2019] is
billed as covering both Western and Eastern traditions, and [Humphreys 2019] observes that
"non-western thought accounts for barely one-eighth of the book and is presented in a rather
tokenistic fashion." Either widen the book or narrow the claim.

### P7. Let proportion carry meaning, and make sure it carries what you intend

Page count is an argument whether or not you mean it to be. If one thinker gets forty pages and
another gets four, the book has said something about their relative importance. Decide what your
proportions are tracking (influence, difficulty, how much the reader needs, how much survives that
is worth reading) and apply it consistently, rather than letting it fall out of which chapters were
easier to write. (synthesis, from [Humphreys 2019] on [Grayling 2019] and the volume-per-tradition
structure of [Adamson 2014- ])

### P8. Define the vocabulary before you lean on it

Every chapter introduces terms the reader does not have: substance, form, *dharma*, *li*, intuition,
the a priori. [Pinker 2014] calls the underlying problem the curse of knowledge, the difficulty of
imagining what it is like for someone else not to know something you know, and identifies it as the
single best explanation of why good people write bad prose. It shows up as underexplaining and as
undefined technical terms the writer no longer notices are technical.

This risk is unusually high in a chronological survey, because the same English word (idea, reason,
nature, virtue) means different things in different centuries and traditions.

### P9. Make the book a door, not a wall

A survey is not a substitute for the primary texts. It succeeds if a reader finishes a chapter
wanting to read Plato and knowing which translation to pick up. That requires real apparatus:
citations that point somewhere, further reading with reasons attached rather than a bare list.
See [`04-structure-and-apparatus.md`](04-structure-and-apparatus.md). (synthesis)

### P10. Do not tidy a thinker into more coherence than they had

Philosophers change their minds, contradict themselves, and leave things unresolved. Presenting a
seamless system where there was not one is what [Skinner 1969] names the **mythology of coherence**.
It is tempting in this format precisely because a chapter wants a shape.

## Failure modes

Named so they can be spotted in a draft.

| Failure | What it looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Doxography** | A parade of names and their views, no reasoning, chapters interchangeable | [Rorty 1984] |
| **Ventriloquism** | The author's judgements delivered in the philosopher's voice, unmarked | [Russell 1945] |
| **The smoothie** | Every position made to sound easy and obvious; no visible difficulty | (synthesis) |
| **Manufactured coherence** | A tidy system imposed on a body of work that had none | [Skinner 1969] |
| **Prolepsis** | "Aristotle anticipated Darwin"; reading later doctrines backwards into a text | [Skinner 1969] |
| **The doctrine myth** | Treating scattered remarks as a settled position, or blaming a writer for not answering a question they never asked | [Skinner 1969] |
| **Tokenism** | A tradition included in the billing but given a fraction of the space | [Humphreys 2019] |
| **Curse of knowledge** | Undefined jargon; steps in an argument skipped because they feel obvious | [Pinker 2014] |
| **The closed book** | No citations, no further reading; the reader has nowhere to go next | (synthesis) |

## Checklist

Run against any chapter draft.

- [ ] Could a reader state this philosopher's argument back, and say where they think it fails?
- [ ] Is every sentence clearly either the philosopher's claim or my comment on it?
- [ ] Have I left the hard parts hard? Is there at least one place where I say why this is difficult
      or contested?
- [ ] Does the chapter say what problem the thinker inherited, and what they left for the next one?
- [ ] Have I taken the strongest reading of the position, not the most dismissible one?
- [ ] Is the length proportionate under the rule I actually chose, not under what was easy to write?
- [ ] Is every technical term defined at first use, in this chapter, without assuming an earlier one
      was read?
- [ ] Have I imposed more system on this thinker than the texts support?
- [ ] Does the chapter point the reader at something to read next, with a reason?
