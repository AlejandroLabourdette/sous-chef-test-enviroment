## D1. Which traditions are in scope

Feeds: everything. This is the first decision.

The research assumes multi-tradition, per the ticket. It does not choose which.

- **Options**: Western plus Indian, Chinese and Arabic-Persian; add Africana; add Japanese, Korean,
  Jewish, Indigenous American; or fewer traditions at greater depth.
- **Consequences**: each tradition added is a fixed share of a fixed word budget (O5 in `02`) and a
  body of primary literature the author must actually engage (D7). [Adamson 2014- ] handles this by
  giving each tradition volume-length treatment rather than a section, which is a length decision as
  much as a scope one.
- **The trap**: adding traditions to the billing without adding proportional space. That is the
  failure [Humphreys 2019] identifies in [Grayling 2019]. **Fewer traditions covered properly beats
  more covered thinly**, and it is more honest.

## D2. Which thinkers get a chapter

Feeds: `02`, `04`. Explicitly out of scope for the research ticket.

- **Options**: inherited canon; canon plus deliberate additions; built from scratch against a stated
  criterion.
- **Consequences**: the roster is the book. [Park 2013] shows the familiar sequence was actively
  reshaped between 1780 and 1830 rather than being a natural fact, so an inherited list is a choice
  too, just an undeclared one.
- **What to decide first**: not the names, the **criterion**. Influence at the time? Influence since?
  Quality of argument? What the reader most needs? Write the criterion down, then apply it, then
  look at what it excluded and decide whether the criterion or the exclusion is wrong.
- Whatever comes out, the note on scope and canon in `04` explains it.

## D3. Total length and format

Feeds: `04`, and D6 and D11 depend on it.

- **Options**: one volume of roughly 150,000-200,000 words, like [Grayling 2019]; a series, like
  [Gottlieb 2000] or [Adamson 2014- ]; a short single volume, like [Warburton 2011].
- **Consequences**: the arithmetic in `04` is unforgiving. Total words divided by thinkers is the
  chapter budget, and below roughly 2,000 words a chapter cannot reconstruct an argument. A series
  removes the constraint and adds the risk of never finishing, which is the visible state of
  [Gottlieb 2000] as a planned three-volume work.
- **Note**: with a multi-tradition scope, a single volume of ordinary length forces either fewer
  traditions (D1) or fewer thinkers per tradition (D2). There is no third way out.

## D4. Where the book starts

Feeds: `02`.

- **Options**: the Presocratics, the default; earlier, in Egypt and Western Asia; simultaneous
  starts in each tradition at its own beginning.
- **Consequences**: the starting point is an argument about what counts as philosophy, made silently
  if not made openly. [Park 2013] documents that histories before the late eighteenth century often
  began in Egypt or Western Asia, and that the reclassification of those traditions as religion
  rather than philosophy was part of the canon's reshaping.
- Whatever the answer, say it in the text rather than just beginning.

## D5. Where the book ends, and whether living philosophers appear

Feeds: `02`, `04`.

- **Options**: stop around 1900; run to the mid twentieth century; include living thinkers, as
  [Warburton 2011] does by closing on Peter Singer.
- **Consequences**: the closer to the present, the harder it is to judge influence, and the more the
  selection becomes an argument about the present rather than a report on the past. Stopping early is
  safer and can read as evasion. A book that ends in 1900 should say why in the text.

## D6. Chapter count

Feeds: `04`. **Blocked on D2 and D3.**

The rule is settled in `04`: derive the count from the word budget and the minimum viable chapter,
not the other way round. The number is not settled and cannot be until D2 and D3 are.

## D7. Source-language policy

Feeds: `03`.

`03` requires working from primary texts and naming translations. For a multi-tradition book the
author will not read every source language, and that is a practical problem, not a moral one. It
still has to be answered explicitly.

- **Options**: work from the best scholarly translations and say so plainly; work from translations
  but have each tradition's chapters read by someone who reads the language (D8); narrow the scope
  (D1) to what can be handled at first or near-first hand.
- **Consequences**: the honest answer is cheap to state and expensive to omit. A stated note that
  chapters on tradition X rest on named translations and named secondary scholarship costs a
  paragraph and forecloses the obvious criticism. Silence invites the charge that the coverage is
  second-hand, which for a global history is the charge that matters most.

## D8. Expert readers per tradition

Feeds: `03`, and D7.

- **Options**: none; informal readers; formally acknowledged consultants.
- **Consequences**: the single highest-leverage quality measure available for the traditions outside
  the author's training, and it directly addresses the accuracy risk in `03`. Costs time, possibly
  money, and schedule. [Pinker 2014] recommends showing drafts to real readers as the remedy for the
  curse of knowledge; the same move applies here for accuracy rather than clarity.

## D9. Transliteration and dating conventions

Feeds: the style sheet in `04`. **Needed before chapter one, not after.**

- **Options**: Pinyin or Wade-Giles for Chinese; IAST with diacritics or simplified transliteration
  for Sanskrit and Pali; conventions for Arabic and Persian; BCE/CE or BC/AD; how uncertain dates are
  marked.
- **Consequences**: cheap now, expensive later. Changing a transliteration scheme after thirty
  chapters is a mechanical edit across the whole book, the glossary, the index and the timeline.
- **Note**: diacritics are more accurate and add friction for the general reader. The audience
  decision (D12) should settle it, and whatever is chosen goes in the style sheet the same day.

## D10. Non-canonical figures: integrated or gathered

Feeds: `02`, `04`.

`02` recommends allowing chapters whose subject is a school, a text or a debate rather than an
individual, and notes that [Adamson 2014- ] deliberately covers the Hippocratic corpus, the Platonic
Academy and the role of women in ancient philosophy.

- **Options**: integrate throughout, in chronological position; gather into dedicated chapters;
  both.
- **Consequences**: integration is truer to the chronology and makes the figures harder to skip.
  Dedicated chapters are more visible and easier to write, and risk the same segregation problem the
  braided structure in `02` exists to avoid. Integration is the better default; dedicated treatment
  is defensible where a group's exclusion is itself the story worth telling.

## D11. Thematic sections per era, or the thread guide alone

Feeds: `04`. **Blocked on D3.**

`04` recommends the thread guide as the affordable option and notes that a substantive thematic
section per era is strictly better and considerably longer, with [Kenny 2004-2007] as the full
version of the idea. Choose once D3 fixes the word budget.

## D12. Audience

Feeds: everything.

**Proposed** in `05`: the serious self-learner, with general-reader readability as a constraint.
Marked there as a proposal. Confirm it or override it, and record the answer here.

## D13. Publishing route

Feeds: `04`, and D3.

- **Options**: trade publisher; academic press; self-publish.
- **Consequences**: a trade publisher constrains length and shapes the apparatus budget, and supplies
  professional indexing and copy-editing. An academic press permits more apparatus and reaches fewer
  general readers. Self-publishing removes both the constraint and the support.
- **Note**: no source in [`sources.md`](sources.md) covers the publishing side. If this decision
  becomes live, it needs its own research; see the gaps section there.

## D14. Title

Feeds: nothing downstream, but constrained by D1. **Blocked on D1.**

The rule is settled: the title has to describe the actual scope. [Garfield & Van Norden 2016] make
the argument in its sharpest form, and [Humphreys 2019] shows what happens when the billing outruns
the contents. An unqualified "history of philosophy" covering only some traditions is the failure;
so is a global claim delivered by a book that is seven-eighths Western.
