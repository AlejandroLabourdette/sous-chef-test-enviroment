# Sources

Annotated bibliography for the philosophy book research. Every entry here has been checked to
exist. Nothing is cited anywhere in this folder that does not appear below.

## How to cite in these documents

Use the short key in square brackets, for example `[Rorty 1984]`. A claim that is not traceable to
a single source is marked `(synthesis)` instead. A claim that is a judgement call rather than a
finding is marked `(judgement)`. If you add a source, verify it first and add it here before you
cite it.

## 1. Historiography and method

How historians of philosophy think about their own job. This is the theoretical backbone: it gives
you named categories for what you are doing and named failure modes for what can go wrong.

### [Rorty 1984]
Richard Rorty, "The Historiography of Philosophy: Four Genres", in *Philosophy in History*, ed.
Richard Rorty, J. B. Schneewind and Quentin Skinner (Cambridge University Press, 1984), ch. 3.

The single most useful piece for this project. Rorty distinguishes four ways of writing about past
philosophy: **historical reconstruction** (what the thinker meant in their own terms and to their
own contemporaries), **rational reconstruction** (treating the thinker as a contemporary and asking
what they would say to us), **Geistesgeschichte** (the story of how the discipline came to ask the
questions it now asks, which is what justifies a canon), and **doxography** (running down a list of
famous names and summarising their views).

Rorty argues the first two are both necessary and that the choice between them is a false dilemma.
He treats doxography as the genre to avoid, and accuses it of mummifying famous philosophers and
ignoring the people who worked at the borders of philosophy, science, politics, economics, morality
and medicine.

- **Good for**: naming what you are writing, chapter by chapter. The doxography warning is aimed
  almost exactly at a chronological survey of great thinkers, which is what this book is.
- **Weak on**: practical craft. It is a methodological essay for academics, not a writing guide.
- Record: https://philpapers.org/rec/RORTHO and
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/philosophy-in-history/historiography-of-philosophy-four-genres/1CAE04EBA26041E43FDCACC6BD2A9BB2

### [Skinner 1969]
Quentin Skinner, "Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas", *History and Theory* 8:1
(1969), 3-53.

The classic statement of what goes wrong when you read past texts through present concerns. Skinner
names three "mythologies":

- **The mythology of doctrines** - treating scattered remarks as if they were the writer's settled
  position on a topic, or criticising a writer for failing to address a question they never asked.
- **The mythology of coherence** - manufacturing a systematic, consistent doctrine out of a body of
  work that is not systematic or consistent.
- **The mythology of prolepsis** - describing a writer as anticipating a later doctrine, which
  confuses the significance an observer finds in a text with what the text actually meant.

- **Good for**: a concrete failure checklist. These three are the standard errors of exactly the
  book being planned.
- **Weak on**: Skinner is strongly contextualist and the essay is polemical; treat it as one side of
  a live argument, not as settled method. See [Rorty 1984] for the counterweight.
- Record: https://philpapers.org/rec/SKIMAU

### [Park 2013]
Peter K. J. Park, *Africa, Asia, and the History of Philosophy: Racism in the Formation of the
Philosophical Canon, 1780-1830* (SUNY Press, 2013). Winner of the 2016 Frantz Fanon Prize.

A historical investigation of how Africa and Asia were written out of the history of philosophy.
Park shows that between 1780 and 1830 European philosophers influenced by Kant reformulated the
history of philosophy as a progression from the Greeks to Kant, replacing earlier accounts that
began in Egypt or Western Asia. Those traditions were then reclassified as religion rather than
philosophy. He argues Kant, Hegel and many historians of philosophy were working with a racialised
philosophical anthropology traceable to Christoph Meiners.

- **Good for**: this is why the standard chronological canon looks the way it does. If the book is
  multi-tradition, this explains what it is departing from and why the departure is a restoration
  rather than an innovation.
- **Weak on**: it is a study of a specific period in German scholarship, not a general history.
- Record: https://philpapers.org/rec/PARAAA-6

### [Garfield & Van Norden 2016]
Jay L. Garfield and Bryan W. Van Norden, "If Philosophy Won't Diversify, Let's Call It What It
Really Is", *The Stone*, The New York Times, 11 May 2016.

Argues that philosophy departments that teach only the European canon should rename themselves
departments of Anglo-European philosophy. The demand is honesty about scope, not obligatory
inclusion.

- **Good for**: the strongest short statement of the principle that scope is a choice you owe the
  reader an explicit account of. Directly applicable: whatever traditions the book covers, say so on
  the tin.
- **Weak on**: it is a short opinion piece, deliberately provocative. Cite it for the argument, not
  as scholarship.

### [Van Norden 2017]
Bryan W. Van Norden, *Taking Back Philosophy: A Multicultural Manifesto* (Columbia University Press,
2017), written in response to the reaction to [Garfield & Van Norden 2016].

- **Good for**: the book-length version of the argument above, with the objections answered.

### [Quine 1960] (principle of charity)
Willard Van Orman Quine, *Word and Object* (MIT Press, 1960), for the underlying idea; the phrase
"principle of charity" was coined by Neil Wilson, and Donald Davidson made it the centrepiece of
radical interpretation, sometimes calling it the principle of rational accommodation.

The principle: interpret a speaker's statements in the most rational way available, and an argument
in its strongest available form, rather than attributing irrationality or obvious falsehood when a
coherent reading exists. Quine's version: one's interlocutor's silliness, beyond a certain point,
is less likely than bad translation. Davidson's: we make maximum sense of the words and thoughts of
others when we interpret in a way that optimises agreement.

- **Good for**: the governing rule for expounding a philosopher you disagree with.
- **Weak on**: charity has a limit and the sources do not draw it for you. See
  `03-faithful-exposition.md`.
- Survey: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_charity and
  https://iep.utm.edu/donald-davidson-mind-action/

## 2. Exemplar histories

Books that have already attempted the format being planned. Read these as worked examples of the
problem, including where they fail.

### [Russell 1945]
Bertrand Russell, *A History of Western Philosophy* (1945).

The famous one-volume chronological survey, and the standard cautionary tale. Criticised for
overgeneralisation and omission, particularly in the post-Cartesian period, and for being "not
always accurate in its account of the thinkers it covers, nor always fair to them". A symposium on
the work made the central charge precisely: a history of philosophy should be an objective statement
of doctrines and arguments, and what Russell actually produced was commentary on the history of
ideas from his own point of view.

- **Good for**: readable, opinionated, commercially successful, and it demonstrates the exact
  trade-off this book has to manage. Also the clearest available illustration of Rorty's rational
  reconstruction pushed until it stops being fair.
- **Weak on**: accuracy and fairness to its subjects. Do not use it as a source of fact about any
  philosopher.
- Record: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_History_of_Western_Philosophy

### [Copleston 1946-1975]
Frederick Copleston, *A History of Philosophy*, 9 volumes (1946-1975).

Written in part to redress what Copleston saw as the wrong done by [Russell 1945]. The aim was to
give full place to each thinker, present the thought in a rounded way, and show the links to
predecessors and successors. Described by scholars and students as broad-minded and objective,
comprehensive and scholarly, unified and well proportioned.

- **Good for**: the opposite pole from Russell, and the source of the "links backward and forward"
  principle that a chronological book lives or dies on.
- **Weak on**: nine volumes. The completeness is bought with a length no general reader will accept,
  and the perspective is Catholic and scholastic-sympathetic.
- Record: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_History_of_Philosophy_(Copleston)

### [Kenny 2004-2007]
Anthony Kenny, *A New History of Western Philosophy*, 4 volumes (Oxford University Press, 2004-2007;
one-volume edition 2010/2012): *Ancient Philosophy*, *Medieval Philosophy*, *The Rise of Modern
Philosophy*, *Philosophy in the Modern World*.

Structurally the most instructive book on this list. Each volume runs a chronological narrative
**and then** revisits the same period through thematic chapters: logic and language, knowledge,
physics, metaphysics, mind and soul, ethics, politics, aesthetics, God. The volumes are designed to
dovetail so a reader can follow one theme across centuries.

- **Good for**: a direct solution to the central structural problem of the genre. Chronology alone
  fragments every theme; this hybrid gives the reader both axes.
- **Weak on**: it doubles the length, and the thematic half necessarily repeats material from the
  narrative half.
- Record: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_New_History_of_Western_Philosophy and
  https://global.oup.com/academic/product/a-new-history-of-western-philosophy-9780199656493

### [Warburton 2011]
Nigel Warburton, *A Little History of Philosophy* (Yale University Press, 2011).

Forty short chapters from Socrates to Peter Singer.

- **Good for**: the clearest available model of the short-chapter, one-thinker-per-chapter,
  general-reader format. If the book wants brevity, this is the exemplar to measure against.
- **Weak on**: depth. Forty chapters covering 2,500 years means a thinker gets a few pages and one
  or two ideas. Also Western-only despite the unqualified title.
- Record: https://philpapers.org/rec/WARALH and
  https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300187793/a-little-history-of-philosophy/

### [Gottlieb 2000] and [Gottlieb 2016]
Anthony Gottlieb, *The Dream of Reason: A History of Philosophy from the Greeks to the Renaissance*
(W. W. Norton, 2000) and *The Dream of Enlightenment: The Rise of Modern Philosophy* (W. W. Norton,
2016). Planned as the first two of three volumes introducing Western philosophy to a wide audience.

- **Good for**: the model of a general-audience history written from the primary texts rather than
  from the secondary literature, at a length that keeps arguments intact.
- **Weak on**: incomplete as a series, and Western-only.
- Record: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dream_of_Reason and
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dream_of_Enlightenment

### [Durant 1926]
Will Durant, *The Story of Philosophy* (Simon & Schuster, 1926).

The commercial ancestor of the whole popular-survey genre, organised as biographical portraits of
major figures.

- **Good for**: evidence that the biography-led approach sells, and a demonstration of how much
  narrative momentum a life story adds.
- **Weak on**: dated, selective, and it is the purest example of the doxographical "great men and
  their views" shape that [Rorty 1984] warns against. Treat as an object of study, not a model.

## 3. Multi-tradition and non-Western histories

The book is planned as global rather than Western-only. These are the works that have tried it and
the tradition-specific references you need so that coverage is not second-hand.

### [Grayling 2019]
A. C. Grayling, *The History of Philosophy* (Viking/Penguin, 2019).

The first accessible single-volume history to attempt Western, Indian, Chinese, Arabic-Persian and
African traditions together, plus an appendix on logic, a timeline of philosophers, a bibliography
and an index.

- **Good for**: the most recent attempt at exactly the book being planned. Its apparatus (timeline,
  logic appendix, bibliography) is a concrete model for `04-structure-and-apparatus.md`.
- **Weak on**: proportion, and this is the important lesson. See [Humphreys 2019].
- Record: https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/610800/the-history-of-philosophy-by-a-c-grayling/

### [Humphreys 2019]
Joe Humphreys, "The History of Philosophy: AC Grayling's insightful but blinkered view", *The Irish
Times*, 25 June 2019.

The review that states the problem in one line: "non-western thought accounts for barely one-eighth
of the book and is presented in a rather tokenistic fashion."

- **Good for**: the concrete proportionality warning. A book billed as covering both Western and
  Eastern traditions while giving one-eighth of its pages to everything outside the West has made a
  promise its structure does not keep. This is the failure mode `02-chronological-format.md` has to
  design against.
- Record: https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/books/the-history-of-philosophy-ac-grayling-s-insightful-but-blinkered-view-1.3919909

### [Adamson 2014- ]
Peter Adamson, *A History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps* (Oxford University Press, 2014 onwards),
based on the podcast at https://historyofphilosophy.net. Volumes include *Classical Philosophy*,
*Philosophy in the Hellenistic and Roman Worlds*, *Philosophy in the Islamic World*, *Medieval
Philosophy*, plus series on India (with Jonardon Ganeri) and Africana philosophy.

The working exemplar for the multi-tradition chronological format. The project is explicitly about
expanding the sense of what the history of philosophy is about, and covers not only major figures
but the Hippocratic corpus, the Platonic Academy, the role of women in ancient philosophy, and
Muslim, Jewish and Christian thinkers in the Islamic world.

- **Good for**: the model to follow. It solves the proportionality problem that [Grayling 2019]
  fails by giving each tradition its own volume-length treatment rather than a section.
- **Weak on**: length and open-endedness. "Without any gaps" is a research programme, not a
  constraint a single book can adopt.
- Record: https://historyofphilosophy.net/hopwag-books and
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Adamson_(philosopher)

### [Ganeri 2017]
Jonardon Ganeri (ed.), *The Oxford Handbook of Indian Philosophy* (Oxford University Press, 2017).
Forty leading scholars on the figures, movements and approaches of philosophy in the Indian
subcontinent.

- **Good for**: the authoritative reference for the Indian material, and a corrective to
  second-hand summaries.
- **Weak on**: it is a scholarly handbook, not a narrative. Not a model for voice.
- Record: https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-oxford-handbook-of-indian-philosophy-9780199314621

### [Fung 1948]
Fung Yu-lan (Feng Youlan), *A Short History of Chinese Philosophy*, ed. Derk Bodde (Macmillan, 1948).
A condensation of his 1934 *History of Chinese Philosophy*, covering Confucianism, Taoism, Mohism,
Yin-Yang and more from antiquity to the twentieth century.

- **Good for**: the standard accessible single-volume history of a non-Western tradition, written
  from inside that tradition. Both a reference and a model of how a survey voice can work.
- **Weak on**: 1948 scholarship, and shaped by Feng's own philosophical commitments.
- Record: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Short_History_of_Chinese_Philosophy and
  https://iep.utm.edu/feng/

### [Berryman 2022]
Sylvia Berryman, "Ancient Atomism", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (first published 2005,
substantive revision 18 October 2022), https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/atomism-ancient/

Covers Greek atomism alongside the Indian Nyaya-Vaisheshika, Buddhist and Jaina atomist traditions,
and is explicit about what can and cannot be claimed regarding contact between them. The tradition
that Democritus knew the ideas of the Indian "gymnosophists" is "difficult to authenticate";
the possibility that Islamic atomism was affected by classical Indian debates has been considered
more seriously, given parallel arguments and the shared view that atoms never exist in isolation.

- **Good for**: the worked example of parallel development versus documented transmission, which is
  the hardest judgement a multi-tradition chronology has to make. Also a model of how to phrase an
  influence claim honestly when the evidence is thin.
- **Weak on**: it is an encyclopedia entry on one topic, not a general treatment of cross-tradition
  influence.

## 4. Craft and style

### [Pinker 2014]
Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style: The Thinking Person's Guide to Writing in the 21st Century*
(Viking, 2014), especially ch. 3, "The Curse of Knowledge".

The curse of knowledge is the difficulty of imagining what it is like for someone else not to know
something you know. Pinker calls it the single best explanation of why good people write bad prose:
it makes writers underexplain, and include undefined jargon and technical terms without noticing.
His remedies are showing drafts to real readers and defining terms rather than assuming them.

- **Good for**: the central writing risk of this specific book. Every chapter is a specialist
  vocabulary the reader does not have yet.
- **Weak on**: it is a general style guide, with nothing specific about philosophy or history.
- Record: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sense_of_Style

## Gaps in this bibliography

Stated honestly so a future session knows what is not covered:

- No source here is a guide to writing a philosophy book specifically. That book does not appear to
  exist; the guidance in this folder is assembled from historiography (section 1), from what the
  exemplars in sections 2 and 3 actually do, and from general craft (section 4). Where it is
  assembled, it is marked `(synthesis)`.
- Africana and Indigenous philosophical traditions are represented here only through [Adamson 2014- ]
  and [Park 2013]. If the book covers them, add tradition-specific references of the same standing
  as [Ganeri 2017] and [Fung 1948].
- No source on the publishing and editorial side: word counts, comparable titles, how trade
  publishers structure this genre.
